WakeKV Phase 0–1: what the experiments do
WakeKV · your research, explained simply

What did we just run on the GPU?

Two cheap experiments that decide whether your research idea deserves months of work. In plain words: do the model's "workers" change their habits in the middle of a job? And if they do — can we see it coming a moment before it happens? The results are in: yes to the first, no (cheaply) to the second — jump to the verdict for what that means.

run the model, take notes→ score the notes (G0)→ test the alarms (G1)
The story so far. A language model is like an office building: every floor (layer) has workers (attention heads), and each worker keeps one folder per word the model has read (the KV cache). Folders eat GPU memory, so researchers throw folders away — but everyone decides whose folders to throw based on how busy each worker looked at the start. Your idea: watch workers during the job, send a quiet worker's folders to the basement (CPU memory) instead of the shredder, and bring them back the moment that worker gets busy again. For that to work, two things must be true — and that's what these experiments test.
1 · the experiment itself

We watch every worker, one word at a time

Normally the model writes its answer and nobody looks inside. Our script (run_phase0.py) makes it write one word at a time, and after every single word it takes a snapshot: which folders did each of the 576 workers open just now? And how much attention did each worker give to the one fact we secretly planted in the text?

Press play. Each square is a worker; amber means "busy reading the planted fact right now". Watch three things: most workers doze the whole time, a couple work non-stop, and a few suddenly get busy halfway through — those late wakers are the reason your idea can exist.

step 0 / 36

Why this doesn't blow up the GPU: we never save the full snapshot — the moment it's taken, we keep only each worker's 64 favourite folders plus two small numbers, and throw the rest away. One full experiment produces a file of only ~100–200 MB.

2 · the three tests

Three jobs, each stressing workers differently

test 1 · niah

Find the hidden code

We bury a secret code inside 5,000 words of boring filler and ask for it at the end. This is the easiest job for workers to stay settled in — so if their habits change even here, they change everywhere.

test 2 · cot

Solve a math problem

A "thinking" model reasons for up to 2,000 words. We watch which workers keep going back to re-read the question mid-solution. Nobody who published competing methods ever tested this situation — it's your home turf.

test 3 · multiturn

Remember the wifi password

We tell the model some facts, chat about pasta and hiking for a while, then ask for the wifi password. Workers must wake up after a long nap to find it — the exact event your system wants to catch.

3 · first question (gate g0)

Scoring the notes: do workers really change?

The analysis script boils each experiment down to a few scores. The main one is simple: compare the busy crew now with the busy crew one word later — how similar are they? A score of 1.0 means the same workers every time (bad news for us: a fixed plan would be fine). A low score means the crew keeps changing (good news: you need a manager who adapts, which is your paper).

There's one trap. A worker's "favourite folders" list can look stable or unstable by pure luck, depending on how many folders exist in total. So we use a luck-corrected score too. Play with both sliders — especially: set the change high and shrink the shelf, and watch the plain score stay flattering while the luck-corrected one calls it out:

what the worker read at word 50
what they read at word 51 (amber = newly picked up)
–plain similarity: how much the two lists agree (1 = identical)
–overlap score: a slightly friendlier version of the same idea
–luck-corrected score: agreement beyond what random picking would give

Fun fact: our very first test run got this wrong — the luck correction was miscalculated, and every worker looked perfectly stable. The numbers disagreeing with each other is what exposed the bug.

And here is what your real first run said (one small model, the easy hidden-code test, only 64 words of output):

≈ 0.5crew similarity word-to-word — the crew changes about half its members between one word and the next. Same range the original research paper found.
6%of workers are restless all the time — matches what a rival paper found (~7%) with different methods. Reassuring.
63%of workers changed their reading habits at least once — occasional change is normal, not rare.

That pattern — a few always-restless workers, a majority who shift occasionally — is exactly the world where a fixed plan fails and an adaptive manager (your system) wins.

4 · defining the moment that matters

When do we say a worker "woke up"?

Scores jiggle constantly — if every jiggle counted as a wake-up, we'd cry wolf all day. So the rule is stricter: a worker must have been properly idle for a while, and then clearly busy — only that counts. Drag the sliders and see which bumps qualify. Notice the two skinny spikes: with sensible settings they're ignored, exactly as they should be.

These confirmed wake-ups become the answer key for the next question: could we have seen each one coming?

5 · second question (gate g1)

Can an alarm ring before the wake-up?

Here's why timing matters. In your future system, a napping worker's folders live in the basement. When the worker wakes, the folders must ride the elevator (the PCIe cable between CPU and GPU) back up. If the alarm rings before the wake-up, the folders arrive in time and nobody waits. If it rings late, the whole model stalls.

We test four possible alarms — each one watches something different about a worker:

alarmin plain words
online_rco"their reading list is changing faster than usual"
drift"they've wandered away from their usual folders"
entropy_trend"their eyes are darting around — searching for something?"
needle_mass_delta"they're already reaching for the fact" (rings latest — used as a reference)

Each alarm gets a report card: out of all the real wake-ups, how many did it warn about (and how early)? And when it rang, how often was it a false alarm? 5b · the honesty test — and what broke

The same alarm can look perfect or useless

This is the twist, and the most important lesson so far. The winning alarm (drift) first looked flawless — it caught every wake-up with no false alarms. Then we checked whether a real system could actually achieve that, and it fell apart. Same alarm, same data — the only thing that changed was how honestly we graded it. Click through the three ways:

–precision — when the alarm rings, how often it's real
–recall — of the real wake-ups, how many it caught
–verdict

The catch in grade 1: we let every worker tune their own alarm sensitivity after already knowing which moments were real wake-ups. No live system can peek at the answers like that. Grade 3 is what a real controller gets — judge each worker against their own normal, decide on the spot — and there, on the hard reasoning task, the best alarm catches only ~4 in 10 wake-ups while crying wolf ~12 times for each real one.

The finding: cheap alarms can't reliably see wake-ups coming. That's not a tuning problem — every alarm we tried lands in the same place. For our system this hurts twice: the misses become stalls, and the false alarms drag workers' folders up to the GPU when they weren't needed — which quietly undoes the memory saving we wanted in the first place.
6 · where we actually landed

The verdict, honestly

g0 · do workers change? — YES

Confirmed on our own models, across all three jobs. On the reasoning task, 85% of workers shifted their reading habits at least once. The problem the project attacks is real. This gate passes.

g1 · see it coming? — NO (cheaply)

No cheap alarm predicts wake-ups well enough to prefetch ahead of time. This gate does not pass in its strong form — an honest, publishable negative result, not a dead end.

So what now — the fork

plan b · react

Fetch on demand, measure the wait

Drop the guessing. When a worker actually reaches for basement folders, fetch them then and honestly report the short stall. Fully supported by the data we have, and a published competitor (FlexiCache) lives with the same stall. Safe, solid, a bit less ambitious.

worth one cheap check · cluster

Do wake-ups arrive in bunches?

Per-worker guessing failed. But if many workers wake together at reasoning turning-points, one coarse "a shift is happening" trigger could catch them cheaply — a better story than any single alarm. A ~20-minute check on data we already have decides it.

Either way, the heart of the idea survives: nobody else moves a worker's folders to the basement and back, live, during the job. The prediction stumble just decides whether that happens by guessing ahead (if wake-ups cluster) or by reacting fast (if they don't).
