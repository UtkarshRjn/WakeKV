       A Case for Reactive KV-Cache Residency}

\begin{abstract}
KV-cache compression methods classify attention heads once, offline or
at prefill, and treat that classification as fixed for the rest of
generation. We measure per-decode-step head activity across three
models (1.5B--8B) and three regimes (needle retrieval, long
chain-of-thought, multi-turn recall) and find 63--85\% of heads shift
their reading habits at least once within a single generation. We
present \emph{WakeKV}, a reactive residency policy that demotes cooling
heads to a CPU reservoir and fetches them back on demand, rather than
committing to a frozen role or destroying evicted state. At page
granularity over the same logged attention, WakeKV is matched exactly
on memory and budget against each comparator rather than compared at
nominal labels: across five real model/regime combinations, it beats a
frozen (FlexiCache-style) policy at every matched-memory point (17/17),
beats destructive eviction at matched budget in 19/20 settings, and
beats three cited baselines (SnapKV, uniform R-KV, ReasonAlloc) at
matched memory in all 32 valid comparisons. Preliminary real-system
measurements (A30, Mistral-7B via a FlexiCache/vLLM shim) confirm the
direction: 1.34--2.33$\times$ throughput at 95--99\% LongBench quality
vs.\ stock FlexiCache.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

KV-cache size dominates long-context LLM serving memory: caching every
attended key/value pair for every head grows linearly in sequence
length and quickly exceeds GPU memory at scale, so a serving system
must decide which parts of the cache to keep resident, compress, or
evict. The dominant response decides each head's treatment once,
offline or at prefill, and holds that decision fixed for the rest of
generation.

Existing approaches split along two axes: \emph{when} a head's role is
decided, and \emph{whether} a demoted head's state can be recovered.
Static head-role methods (DuoAttention~\cite{duoattention},
RazorAttention~\cite{razorattention}, MoA~\cite{moa},
HeadKV~\cite{headkv}, FlexiCache~\cite{flexicache},
HeteroCache~\cite{heterocache}) fix treatment offline or at prefill;
adaptive allocators (Ada-KV~\cite{adakv}, CAKE~\cite{cake},
LAVa~\cite{lava}, DynamicKV~\cite{dynamickv}) remain prefill-only
despite the framing. Decode-time compressors (R-KV~\cite{rkv},
G-KV~\cite{gkv}, ThinKV~\cite{thinkv}, SCOPE~\cite{scope}) update during
generation but evict destructively; ReasonAlloc~\cite{reasonalloc}
reallocates per-head budgets online but still destroys KV on demotion.
Offload systems (ArkVale~\cite{arkvale}, ShadowKV~\cite{shadowkv},
InfiniGen~\cite{infinigen}, HeadInfer~\cite{headinfer}, FlexiCache,
HeteroCache) keep demoted KV recoverable but never revisit head
priority at runtime. No prior system is both \emph{dynamic} and
\emph{reversible} at once.

Recent measurement work on retrieval heads \cite{retrievalheadsdynamic}
casts doubt on this shared premise: attention-head importance is
non-stationary even within a single generation, and that work names
dynamic per-head KV retention as an open direction. We close that gap
with \emph{WakeKV} (\S\ref{sec:method}), a reactive residency policy
that never permanently discards a head's state. We validate it at page
granularity by replaying real, logged decode-time attention through
full/frozen/reactive/evict policies and three cited baseline
reimplementations, comparing every policy at matched memory or matched
budget rather than a shared nominal label.

Our contributions include:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Head non-stationarity:} Replicated and extended across
three models, including two regimes that competing frozen-role methods
do not evaluate (long CoT, multi-turn).
\item \textbf{Signal study:} Cheap per-head signals cannot predict
wake-ups well enough to prefetch, though a structurally different,
data-driven signal (generated-token identity) does in CoT specifically;
wake-ups are real but not tightly detectable enough for a coarse
trigger.
\item \textbf{Residency simulator:} At page granularity, reactive LRU +
fetch-on-demand dominates frozen classification, and \emph{reversible}
offload specifically (not just dynamism) beats destructive eviction and
three cited baselines, across five real model/regime combinations.
\item \textbf{Real-system confirmation:} On A30/Mistral-7B via a
FlexiCache/vLLM shim, WakeKV reactive beats stock throughput at every
rerank interval ($1.34$--$2.33\times$) while holding $95$--$99\%$ of
LongBench quality, confirming the simulator-level direction on real
hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\section{WakeKV}
\label{sec:method}

WakeKV replaces per-head classification with a single runtime rule.
Each head keeps a GPU-resident set, LRU-ordered and capped at a
per-head budget $B$; a page evicted under budget pressure is demoted to
a CPU reservoir rather than destroyed, and is fetched back the moment
it is wanted again (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). A wrong or stale
classification thus costs one recoverable stall, not a permanent
accuracy loss: the combination no prior static or destructive method
offers (\S\ref{sec:intro}). \S\ref{sec:results} compares this reactive
rule against a frozen (FlexiCache-style) policy and an \emph{evict}
foil sharing reactive's exact LRU schedule but destroying instead of
reserving.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}

We evaluate on three models, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, across
three regimes: NIAH (needle retrieval, $\approx$5000-token filler),
long CoT (MATH-500~\cite{math500}, up to 2048 generated tokens), and
multi-turn recall. At every decode step and head we log the top-$k$
attended positions and weights ($k=64$), along with a continuous
needle score and a binary copy-paste score following
\cite{retrievalheadsdynamic}'s Eq.~1--2. The 1.5B and 3B models were
measured on an RTX~2080~Ti (11GB); the 8B model and the real-system
follow-up ran on an A30 (24GB).

\subsection{Churn Measurement}

Let $A_t$ denote the set of heads
(or top-$k$ pages) a given head attends to at decode step $t$. We track
step-to-step stability with the adjacent-step Jaccard index:
\begin{equation}
J(A_t, A_{t+1}) \;=\; \frac{|A_t \cap A_{t+1}|}{|A_t \cup A_{t+1}|}.
\label{eq:jaccard}
\end{equation}
$J=1$ means the attended set is unchanged between consecutive steps;
$J=0$ means complete turnover. Measured adjacent-step Jaccard is $0.53$
(continuous score, NIAH), within \cite{retrievalheadsdynamic}'s reported
$0.28$--$0.51$ range (Table~\ref{tab:churn}).

``Unstable'' heads (Table~\ref{tab:churn}) are identified with
FlexiCache's temporal-stability statistic, Random-Corrected Overlap,
which discounts raw top-$K$ overlap by its chance level under a
candidate pool of size $N$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RCO}(A,B) \;=\; \max\!\left(0,\; \frac{\nicefrac{|A\cap B|}{K} - \nicefrac{K}{N}}{1 - \nicefrac{K}{N}}\right), \qquad K = \min(|A|,|B|).
\label{eq:rco}
\end{equation}
$\mathrm{RCO}=0$ at chance-level overlap and $\mathrm{RCO}=1$ when the
two sets coincide exactly. A head is flagged unstable when its mean RCO
over a trailing 16-step window falls below $0.5$. Under this test, the
fraction of heads with $\geq 1$ HeteroCache-style drift event (a
windowed-median overlap-vs-baseline dropping below $\tau{=}0.5$; the
baseline resets after each event) ranges $0.63$--$0.85$ across regimes
and holds at 8B ($0.79$ on CoT), where a small chronically-unstable tail
($2$--$9\%$) coexists with a large majority of occasionally-shifting
heads.

\subsection{Wake-up Prediction}

We grade four candidate signals (page-overlap change, windowed-median
drift, entropy trend, needle-mass delta) under a \emph{causal} per-head
z-score, using only the trailing $w{=}32$-step window's mean and
standard deviation (online-computable):
\begin{equation}
z_t \;=\; \frac{x_t - \mu_{t-w:t}}{\sigma_{t-w:t}}.
\label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
The best signal achieves precision $0.08$--$0.20$ at recall
$0.41$--$0.70$; an ensemble (vote $\geq 2$ of 4) never beats the single
best signal. A structurally different candidate does clear a bar:
whether the just-generated token predicts a wake burst
(Bonferroni-corrected, held out) reaches F1 $0.53$--$0.62$ on CoT,
$4$--$5\times$ every attention-derived signal, but zero markers survive
on NIAH/multi-turn. Wake-ups are also bursty ($z=+9.7$ vs.\ shuffle
null on CoT) but not tightly enough for a coarse trigger (54\% caught
at a 2\%-of-steps trigger rate). Both cheap proactive routes are ruled
out.

\subsection{Reactive vs.\ Frozen and Evict}

We simulate, at page granularity over
the same logged attention: \emph{full} (ceiling), \emph{frozen}
(FlexiCache-style, offline stable/unstable split)\footnote{Threshold and
cadence parameters throughout are each baseline's own published
configuration, not tuned by us: frozen's 25\% unstable-head split and
16-step stable-head refresh are FlexiCache's; the 128-step recompression/
reallocation cadence for SnapKV/R-KV/ReasonAlloc below is each paper's
own default. Only the per-head budget sweep itself is our own design
axis.}, \emph{reactive}
(WakeKV: LRU-capped per-head budget, CPU-reservoir fetch-on-demand,
nothing destroyed), and \emph{evict} (identical LRU schedule to
reactive, same budget, but destructive demotion, isolating
reversibility as a variable). Sweeping the per-head budget $B$ traces
each policy's own (memory, miss-rate) curve; since frozen and reactive
reach different memory at the same nominal $B$, we interpolate
reactive's curve, piecewise-linearly between its two bracketing
measured points, onto each of frozen's $17$ actually-measured memory
values (one per swept $B$, pooled across the five combos; $3$ of the
$20$ possible points are dropped where reactive's own sweep never
reached that much memory, not a loss). At each of those $17$ real,
matched memory levels, reactive's interpolated miss rate is $\leq$
frozen's: \textbf{17/17}. At matched budget (no interpolation needed, both
share the identical LRU cap), reactive beats evict in \textbf{19/20}
settings; the one exception (8B/CoT, tightest budget) is explained by
resident-set composition drift after the first page reactive recovers
that evict destroyed, not a flaw in the comparison.

\subsection{Baseline Comparison}

We further implement three faithful, cited reimplementations:
SnapKV~\cite{snapkv} (prefill selection, frozen through decode),
uniform R-KV~\cite{rkv} (periodic destructive pruning), and
ReasonAlloc~\cite{reasonalloc} (online reallocation with a starvation
floor), each with a declared simplification where logged data doesn't
support the full method (e.g.\ no redundancy term, raw keys aren't
logged). Comparing at the same nominal budget label is misleading
here, since these three prune periodically and can silently use
several times the memory; under the same matched-memory correction
above, reactive wins \textbf{32/32} valid comparisons. (Mistral-7B/NIAH
vs.\ R-KV/ReasonAlloc is excluded: the 128-step pruning interval never
triggered on that model's short NIAH answers.)

\subsection{Real-Hardware Deployment}

A FlexiCache/vLLM shim
(two config overrides: zero ``stable'' heads, rerank every step) turns
stock FlexiCache into WakeKV reactive. On Mistral-7B (LEval $8$k
$\to$ $1000$, A30), reactive beats stock throughput at every rerank
interval (Table~\ref{tab:m2b2}) while holding $95$--$99\%$ of LongBench
quality with no monotonic erosion. Part of the win is less attention
work per step (0 vs.\ 64 dense heads), not purely memory management:
holding rerank frequency fixed at stock's cadence, 0 dense heads alone
yields $2.33\times$ throughput for a 5-point quality cost.

\section{Limitations}

Scout-scale models (1.5B--8B); five CoT runs (407 wake events) but
only one multi-turn run (73 events, coarser). The 2602.11162 binary
retrieval score is thresholded, so adjacent-step Jaccard reads higher
under it than under the continuous score; we report both in
Table~\ref{tab:churn}. The simulator counts \emph{misses}, not stall
\emph{time}, at page granularity from top-$k$, against
FlexiCache-\emph{style} policies, not their exact code. The
real-system result is narrow (single model/regime, one A30) and
preliminary. Real-system reversible-vs-evict and baseline comparisons
need patching FlexiCache's continuous-batching internals (designed,
not implemented). Broader coverage is future work.
